The problem isn't always revenue

You're busy. The business is growing. So where's the money?

Your customers are calling. Your team is working. You've got a bookkeeper, maybe a controller, and someone who can pull a job-cost report.

But the question that really matters is rarely answered by a report alone:

What should we do differently next month so more of that revenue actually turns into profit and cash?

Questions worth asking about your business

  • We did more work this year. Why didn't we make more money?
  • We know the job came in over budget. How do we stop that from happening again?
  • We're profitable on paper. Why am I watching the bank balance every Friday?
  • Could we raise prices, or would we lose the work?
  • Can we afford to hire another crew? And what happens if a big customer pays late?

These are management questions, not bookkeeping questions. They are the reason Kells exists.

Our point of view

Don't pay us to tell you what happened. Pay us to help change what happens next.

Accounting records the numbers. Job-costing software organizes them. We look across that information for the decisions that could improve your economics.

Some opportunities are pricing. Others are labor planning, scope changes, customer mix, overhead, collections or timing. We follow the economics rather than sell the same answer to every company.

What finding profit actually looks like

A job can show a profit and still be leaving money behind.

Take a specialty contractor. The accounting system shows what each project earned. The missing piece is understanding why similar jobs perform differently, and what the team should change before the next estimate goes out.

12 similar installation jobsIllustrative example / invented numbers
Estimated labor per job420 hours
Actual average labor per job495 hours
Unplanned labor per job75 hours
Burdened labor cost$48 / hour
Extra labor cost across 12 jobs$43,200
75 extra hours × $48 × 12 jobs = $43,200. This example demonstrates the method, not a Kells client result.

The extra cost isn't the answer. It's the start of the investigation.

  1. Where did the hours go? Poor scoping, second site visits, waiting on other trades, rework, or something else?
  2. Which jobs share the pattern? Same estimator? Same customer? Same type of site?
  3. What could change? Different scheduling, clearer scope language, a revised labor standard, or a pricing adjustment?
  4. Did it work? Compare the next set of similar jobs against the original baseline.

If the company could eliminate half those unplanned hours, that would model to $21,600 less labor cost across 12 comparable jobs.

Whether that improvement is achievable, and whether it translates fully into additional profit, depends on the operational cause, capacity, and actual cost behavior. We would validate those factors with the people doing the work.

The distinction: your job-cost report says the work ran over budget. Kells helps you work out why, whether it's fixable, how much it matters, and who should do what next.
The actual work

Six places we look for better economics.

Not six products to buy. Six ways a business can earn more, protect cash, or avoid an expensive decision. We start where the evidence points.

01

Make more on each job.

Look across estimates, actual hours, materials, subcontractors, change orders and callbacks. Find repeat patterns rather than simply naming the worst-performing job.

The decisionChange an estimating standard, scope a recurring cost explicitly, fix a process, or stop accepting certain jobs on the old terms.
02

Price the work you already do more intelligently.

Compare realized margin by customer, service and job type. Understand which discounts create volume and which merely give away profit. Explore what customers actually value and which price changes are testable.

The decisionWhere can pricing, minimums, surcharges, contract terms or customer selection change, without assuming every customer will accept a higher price?
03

Find labor and capacity losses.

Separate billable time from travel, waiting, rework, idle capacity and avoidable overtime. Assess whether the issue is workload, planning, utilization or pricing.

The decisionShould you add staff, schedule differently, subcontract selectively, or make current capacity more productive first?
04

Challenge costs without cutting what matters.

Examine recurring software, vehicles, insurance, vendors, materials, purchasing practices and overhead allocation. Find costs that no longer match how the business operates.

The decisionWhat is genuinely avoidable? What should be renegotiated? Where would a cheap cut actually cost more later?
05

Stop being surprised by cash.

Build a 13-week view of expected collections, payroll, vendor payments, taxes, debt service and commitments. See the effect of delayed receipts and growth decisions.

The decisionWhen should you follow up on receivables, adjust planned spending, or prepare an available financing option?
06

Know which growth is worth funding.

Model another crew, a new truck, an equipment purchase, a larger customer contract or a new location against incremental margin, upfront cash and downside risk.

The decisionWill growth leave you with more profit and cash after the additional resources it requires?
Profit isn't the same thing as cash

Payroll is due Friday. Your customers have their own schedule.

You can have a profitable month and still run short of cash. A forward-looking forecast shouldn't just predict a balance. It should tell you when you may need to act.

WeekExpected cashIf a receipt is late
Week 3$145,000$145,000
Week 4$190,000$100,000
Week 5$150,000$60,000
Week 6$135,000$45,000
Week 7$175,000$85,000
Week 8$200,000$200,000
Entirely illustrative figures. Assume the company's agreed cash reserve is $75,000 and the delayed receipt arrives in week 8.

That $45,000 is a decision trigger.

Against a $75,000 reserve, the week-six scenario identifies a $30,000 potential gap before it happens.

  • Which customer collections could be accelerated?
  • Which spending is discretionary, and which payments can be rescheduled by agreement?
  • Is a credit line available, and what would interest and fees cost under the likely draw period?
  • Would hiring or buying equipment now create a deeper shortfall?

This is the goal: fewer Friday surprises, more choices while you still have time to make them.

Questions we'd expect

Before you spend time talking to us.

We already have a bookkeeper or CPA. Why would we need this?

They may be doing exactly what you hired them to do. We focus on a different question: what could the business change to improve future profitability and cash? We start with their information and work alongside them rather than duplicating their work.

How can you help with pricing if you don't know our industry?

We don't set prices from a generic template. We analyze historical costs, quoted and realized margins, customer and job patterns, and competitive constraints as you understand them. We test possible changes with you and measure results. Industry expertise remains essential, and it often comes from your team.

Does this require new software?

No. We start with your accounting, estimating, payroll and other existing data where available. We may build working forecasts or analysis tools to support ongoing decisions, but we're not selling an expensive new system as a prerequisite.

How much does an assessment cost?

We quote a fixed fee once we understand the question and scope. A narrow review of one service line isn't the same job as reconstructing years of inconsistent records. You'll know what you're buying before you commit.

Can you guarantee higher profit?

No responsible advisor can guarantee a particular improvement before examining the business. We can commit to a disciplined process, transparent assumptions and practical recommendations. The size and feasibility of opportunities depend on the facts and your team's ability to implement changes.

What if our data isn't great?

We'll tell you what can be concluded reliably and what can't. Some issues can be investigated with limited information; others may first require better job coding or a cleanup by your accounting team. We won't dress uncertain numbers up as facts.

What do I need for the first call?

Just a brief description of the business and where you'd most like more clarity. Please don't put confidential statements, account numbers or customer data into the contact form. We can agree on a secure exchange method if we work together.
